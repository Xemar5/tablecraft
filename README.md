# Tablecraft

A local-first, 2D board-game design and playtesting workshop. Built with React, TypeScript, Vite and SpaceTimeDB 2.15, with a shared validated command engine for offline and online play.

## Run

Use Node.js **22.23.4+ on the 22.x line**, **24.22.1+ on the 24.x line**, or **26.12.1+**, as required by the SpaceTimeDB SDK.

```powershell
npm ci
npm run dev
```

Starting the development server automatically opens **http://127.0.0.1:5173** in your browser. The component designer and hotseat playground work without a backend, account or multiplayer connection. The included Wildwood project is a fully editable example, not a fixed game. It doubles as a tour of the app: spirit cards, amber tokens, a die and a clearing board built from CSV rows and one linked botanical artwork (fitted inside each frame, with a boolean-cut moon), whose names and rules shrink their font to fit (try The elder oak of the deep wood) and whose power number grows to the left from its bottom-right pivot; a persistent amber supply; the **Wanderer's path**, a board carrying its stepping stones as a points grid that travels with it and only takes the two wanderers (by grid tag), under a faint table grid; a book, the **Wanderer's guide**, that explains each part; and a **Gather amber** button whose Lua script draws amber into your hand and counts it in memory for the **Amber tally**. The path (a locked group), the wanderers and the guide and its controls are filed in Pieces-panel groups. Its card, token, board and dice are also the **Add component** templates.

### Project home and reusable components

Click the **Tablecraft logo** to open Home. One project is loaded at a time. Home shows it under **Open project**, with **Continue** to return to it and **Close** to close it, apart from your other **Projects**, each with **Load**. Loading another project (or creating one, or joining a table) closes the loaded one first. Whenever the loaded project has changes since its last snapshot (Home marks it **Unsaved changes**), closing it asks whether to save them as a snapshot, discard them or cancel. A loaded project stays loaded, unsaved changes included, when you reload the page, and the app opens it again; with nothing loaded, the app starts on Home. **Close project** is also in the header's **Actions** menu. Home lets you search projects (by name, description or component name; Escape clears the search), show them as a **List** or a **Grid** of tiles with previews of their first components (the view switch beside the search box, remembered in this browser), create or load projects, expand their component lists (in the list), give projects an optional description when creating them (shown under the project name), edit a project's name and description with its pencil button, import/export individual projects or the full workspace, delete projects with confirmation, and reorder them by dragging their grips (or focus a grip and use Up/Down, or the arrow keys in the grid; reordering is off while searching). Deleting the last project leaves an empty workspace. Clicking Home while connected leaves the live room and retains its latest state locally.

**New project** can start blank or from a ready-to-play game under **Start from**; picking a game fills in its name and description until you edit them, and the project opens straight onto its set-up table. Every piece is an ordinary component you can edit. Each side's or player's pieces are filed in a named group in the **Pieces** panel, and parts that never move during play are locked, so you can drag and box-select pieces over them (hover a locked piece and press **L** to unlock it).

- **Wildwood**, the sample tour of Tablecraft described under Run (the same project a new workspace starts with): start a fresh copy of it at any time. Its clearing and the Wanderer's path sit in locked groups whose order is locked too (so they stay under everything played on them), and the spirit deck and amber supply are persistent stacks.
- **Chess**, **Checkers** (flip a checker to crown it king) and **Go** (19×19, with stone bowls; Shift-drag a stone off its bowl) for two players. Chess and Checkers show a 60 px grid and snap pieces to the centres of the squares; Go snaps stones onto the line crossings. Tidewater Isles has no square grid, since its hex tiles don't sit on one; it has its own grids (see Grids under Playing). Chess and Checkers boards and the chess pieces turn in half turns (Q/E), since the two players face each other. Chess pieces and Go stones have the same design on their backs, so double-sided printing doesn't leave them blank. Each board sits in its own locked **Board** group, with its order locked so it stays under the pieces (unlock the group in the Pieces panel to move it), and Go's stone bowls are locked inside their players' groups, with their order locked too, with each player's stones a persistent stack on their bowl; each side's pieces have their own group.
- **Tidewater Isles**, an original 3–4 player game of settling tidal islands: 19 hex island tiles (each shows its terrain and resource above a round slot for its tide marker, and flips to a sea back, so you can stack, shuffle and deal a new island), numbered tide markers, nine harbor piers set flush against coastal tile edges (each turned out to sea, with a post on the two corners it serves), huts, lighthouses and boardwalks in four colors, resource and tide card decks, awards, building-cost cards, the squall, two dice and a rule book. The **Island** group holds the open sea, the laid-out tiles, the tide markers in their slots and the harbors, each in its own locked subgroup (unlock a subgroup in the Pieces panel to rearrange it, for example to deal a random island; the tiles, tide markers and harbors still show their hover preview while locked), plus the squall on the dunes. The whole Island group has its order locked, so moving the squall doesn't raise it over the huts and boardwalks or move the island up the Pieces panel. The **Supply** group holds the resource piles, tide card deck, awards, dice and rules (the rule book's order is locked); each player's huts, lighthouses, boardwalks and locked building-cost card (it still shows its hover preview, and its order is locked) form a group in their color. Huts, lighthouses and boardwalks have the same design on their backs and harbors a bare pier, so double-sided printing doesn't leave them blank. The resource piles, the tide card deck and every player's hut, lighthouse and boardwalk piles are persistent stacks, so each stays on the table as an empty slot when it runs out; each pile's filter takes only its own kind back (huts into hut piles, resources into resource piles). Island tiles, harbors and boardwalks turn in 60° steps so they stay on the hex grid (boardwalks start along a tile edge), and huts and lighthouses in 30° steps, so they can face any seat and still line up with tile corners. Its **Tidewater rules** script (see Scripting) runs the dice and the resources. The **Controls** group holds a **Start game** button, a **Roll dice** button and a scoreboard, all locked (the buttons still press), in a group whose order is locked. The script hides the buttons that don't apply from every seat: **Start game** once the game is under way, and **Roll dice** until then and while `autoRoll` is on (turning it off in the Memory window shows it). Before the game starts, the script records each hut that snaps onto a corner. **Start game** first checks every seat's setup: two huts on corners of the island and two boardwalks on its edges, each hut with one of its boardwalks beside it, no huts on neighbouring corners and no lighthouses yet. If anything is off it pings the pieces involved, announces what each player still has to fix and doesn't start (set `checkSetup` to false in the Memory window to start anyway). Then it gives every player one resource for each tile touching their second hut. From then on the dice roll by themselves whenever a turn starts: every hut on a corner of a tile showing the roll takes one of that tile's resource and every lighthouse two, from the resource cards on the table into the owner's hand, unless the squall is on the tile. A 7 announces the squall instead, with the players who must discard half their cards. Moving the squall onto a tile names the players beside it to take a card from. The scoreboard shows the last roll, the round and each colour's building points (huts 1, lighthouses 2). The script's memory holds `colors` (the colour each seat plays, in seat order), `autoRoll` (false to roll only with the button), `started`, `round`, `lastRoll`, `points` and `placed`.

In the component library, choose **Link from another project**, then select a source component. The component and its nested dependencies remain read-only links, not independent designs. The dialog groups components by source project, each with a kind icon and a **Link** button. Library badges show their source; opening one shows a read-only preview. **Make local copy** creates an independently editable copy of the complete nested dependency bundle with fresh IDs, leaving the original link and existing placed pieces intact.

Local source edits refresh linked snapshots, including placed and held pieces, and source changes are picked up when opening a project or restarting the app. Cross-project dependency cycles are rejected. Project/component exports and live rooms include the full read-only snapshot and provenance, so they render without access to the source workspace. If a source project or component is deleted or unavailable, the last snapshot remains usable. Importing a full workspace remaps internal project links to the imported projects. Source projects are browser-local, not remotely subscribed: multiplayer participants receive the room's snapshots; explicitly selecting the link again refreshes a live room from your locally available source. Read-only is an editing constraint, not a security or secrecy guarantee.

## Agent CLI

The CLI uses the same project schemas and command engine as the app. After `npm ci`, run `npm --silent run cli -- --help`. Successful commands print JSON on stdout (except help); errors print JSON to stderr and exit nonzero. Use npm's `--silent` option to avoid its banner on stdout. Project and component files are ZIP archives containing `project.json` and embedded asset files. Command and component-override inputs remain UTF-8 JSON. These examples use PowerShell:

```powershell
# Create an offline archive and add a component, including its linked dependencies.
npm --silent run cli -- new --out game.tablecraft.zip --template blank --name "My game"
npm --silent run cli -- generate --file game.tablecraft.zip --template card --name "My cards"
npm --silent run cli -- list --file game.tablecraft.zip --what components
npm --silent run cli -- schema --what command
npm --silent run cli -- inspect --file game.tablecraft.zip --component COMPONENT_ID
npm --silent run cli -- spawn --file game.tablecraft.zip --component COMPONENT_ID --count 3 --x 700 --y 450
npm --silent run cli -- apply --file game.tablecraft.zip --commands commands.json --actor PLAYER_ID
npm --silent run cli -- validate --file game.tablecraft.zip
npm --silent run cli -- export --file game.tablecraft.zip --out card.component.zip --component COMPONENT_ID
npm --silent run cli -- import --file game.tablecraft.zip --bundle card.component.zip
npm --silent run cli -- export --file game.tablecraft.zip --out portable.tablecraft.zip

# Organise table pieces into (nested) Pieces panel groups.
npm --silent run cli -- group create --file game.tablecraft.zip --name "Market" --pieces PIECE_ID,PIECE_ID
npm --silent run cli -- group create --file game.tablecraft.zip --name "Shared" --groups GROUP_ID
npm --silent run cli -- group rename --file game.tablecraft.zip --group GROUP_ID --name "Supply"
npm --silent run cli -- group move --file game.tablecraft.zip --pieces PIECE_ID --parent GROUP_ID
npm --silent run cli -- group ungroup --file game.tablecraft.zip --group GROUP_ID
npm --silent run cli -- list --file game.tablecraft.zip --what groups

# Lock pieces in place (or unlock them) by piece or by group.
npm --silent run cli -- lock --file game.tablecraft.zip --groups GROUP_ID
npm --silent run cli -- unlock --file game.tablecraft.zip --pieces PIECE_ID,PIECE_ID
# Lock a group itself, leaving each piece's own lock as it is.
npm --silent run cli -- group lock --file game.tablecraft.zip --group GROUP_ID
npm --silent run cli -- group lock --file game.tablecraft.zip --group GROUP_ID --order

# List the grid layers, change the first one, or add, change and remove grids and snap points.
npm --silent run cli -- grid --file game.tablecraft.zip
npm --silent run cli -- grid --file game.tablecraft.zip --show on --snap on --size 60 --offset-x 50 --offset-y 40
npm --silent run cli -- grid add --file game.tablecraft.zip --shape hex --name Island --size 97 --orientation pointy --tags tile
npm --silent run cli -- grid add --file game.tablecraft.zip --shape points --id spots --name Spots --radius 30
npm --silent run cli -- grid point --file game.tablecraft.zip --grid spots --x 400 --y 300 --rotation 90 --label Start
npm --silent run cli -- grid set --file game.tablecraft.zip --grid spots --snap off
npm --silent run cli -- grid remove --file game.tablecraft.zip --grid spots

# Lua scripts and their shared memory (see Scripting).
npm --silent run cli -- script set --file game.tablecraft.zip --name Rules --source rules.lua
npm --silent run cli -- script list --file game.tablecraft.zip
npm --silent run cli -- call --file game.tablecraft.zip --function nextRound --actor PLAYER_ID --args "[1]"
npm --silent run cli -- memory --file game.tablecraft.zip --path score.red --set 3
npm --silent run cli -- memory --file game.tablecraft.zip
```

Replace IDs with those in `list`/`inspect` output. `grid` prints the grid layers (first = highest priority); without a subcommand its `--show`, `--snap`, `--size`, `--offset-x` and `--offset-y` change the first grid as before. `grid add --shape square|hex|points` and `grid set --grid ID` take `--name`, `--show/--snap on|off`, `--size`, `--size-y` (square cell height), `--offset-x/--offset-y`, `--orientation pointy|flat`, `--rotation DEG|none`, `--radius` (snap distance of points grids) and `--tags A,B` (the grid takes pieces with any of these grid tags; `none` takes every piece); `grid point` adds (or, with an existing `--id`, replaces) a snap point and `grid remove` deletes a grid. `new` also supports `--template wildwood` and the ready-to-play games `chess`, `checkers`, `go` and `tidewater` (seated with a "Player 1" profile); `generate` supports `blank`, `card`, `token`, `board`, `dice`, `book`, `artwork` and `button`. `script set` adds a script or replaces the one with that name (running `onLoad`), `script remove` deletes one, and `script list` prints the scripts, the functions they define, memory and the latest console lines. `call` runs a global function (as the `--actor` seat, with optional `--piece` and a JSON `--args` list) and prints memory and the new console lines, failing with the script error if it fails. `memory` prints memory or the value at `--path` (keys separated by dots, list positions from 1) and changes it with `--set JSON` or `--remove`. `group` commands take comma-separated IDs; a piece always brings its whole stack, `--parent root` moves to the top level, `--id` picks the new group's ID, and groups that end up empty are removed. `list --what groups` includes each group's pieces (recursively). `lock` and `unlock` take `--pieces` and/or `--groups` (every piece in those groups, including subgroups); pieces bring their whole stack, pieces already in that state are left alone, and the result lists the IDs that changed. They use the shared `move` command with a `locked` patch; with `--order` they lock or unlock the pieces' places in the order instead (an `orderLocked` patch), and `group lock --order` does the same for groups. The same actions are the shared `createGroup`, `renameGroup`, `ungroup` and `moveToGroup` commands, and `{"type":"cycleVariant","ids":[...],"delta":1}` turns book pages or steps dice faces. Supply `--data component-overrides.json` to merge a JSON object of component fields over the template (including `front`, `back`, `csv`, and `rows`); schema and cross-reference validation still apply. Component exports include dependencies and imports remap their IDs. `commands.json` is a command object or array, for example `[{"type":"settings","patch":{"snap":true}}]`; inspect the command schema for every supported action. Offline `apply` is atomic and supports `{"type":"undo"}` for prior playground commands **in the same batch only**; its history is not persisted across CLI invocations. Use `--project PROJECT_ID` to select a project in a multi-project archive. Mutations replace the input file atomically only after the whole batch validates. New/export destinations are never overwritten without `--force`.

For live rooms, first start and publish the server as described below, then:

```powershell
npm --silent run cli -- live host --file game.tablecraft.zip --code MY-TABLE --name "Agent"
npm --silent run cli -- live state --code MY-TABLE
npm --silent run cli -- live dispatch --code MY-TABLE --commands commands.json
npm --silent run cli -- live undo --code MY-TABLE
# Another identity can instead use: npm --silent run cli -- live join --code MY-TABLE
```

Each invocation joins (or rejoins) the room; `state` reads the current full state, and `dispatch` submits the same validated commands to authoritative reducers. After joining, the CLI takes a seat if its identity has none: the `--actor` seat when it is empty, otherwise the first empty seat, otherwise a new one. Occupied seats cannot be claimed, and the CLI's seat is freed when it disconnects. Live batches commit **one command at a time**; on error the confirmed count is reported, and a timed-out submitted operation may have committed—read state before retrying. Undo uses the server's shared playground history. `--uri`, `--database`, and bounded `--timeout` (milliseconds, 100–120000) are available; defaults are `http://127.0.0.1:3000`, `tablecraft-studio`, and 12000. Non-loopback connections require HTTPS/WSS. `--name` and `--color` configure the seat it takes. Hosting fails if the code exists; joining fails if it does not.

The CLI keeps a separate durable identity per server/database under `~/.tablecraft/identities`, outside Git repositories. The directory/files are created with owner-only permissions where supported; on Windows keep the user profile private. These tokens are secrets: do not copy them into projects, logs, or source control. The CLI identity is separate from the browser identity. Like the browser, it has one active room at a time and receives complete room state; see the trust model below.

**Artwork** is a component type for reusable, linked illustrations, especially nested artwork in cards, tokens, and boards. Choose **Create component → Artwork** (or CLI `generate --template artwork`) for a blank, transparent 200 × 200 canvas ready for editable vector layers. Artwork starts without hand, flip, stack, or shuffle support; existing component templates keep their defaults. The library's **Artwork** filter includes Wildwood's botanical artwork. Add an artwork component with **Add linked artwork** (link icon) to reuse its live design and CSV bindings; exports include linked dependencies. The component JSON/API kind is `"artwork"`, also exposed by the CLI component schema.

## Multiplayer

Install the [SpaceTimeDB CLI](https://spacetimedb.com/install), then:

```powershell
# Terminal 1: persistent local server
spacetime start --listen-addr 127.0.0.1:3000

# Terminal 2: dependencies, build and publish
Set-Location server
npm ci
Set-Location ..
npm run db:publish
```

Publish using a persistent CLI identity, not `--anonymous`, so you can update the database later. If the CLI prompts for authentication, follow its login flow. For local-only development, you can obtain a server-issued identity without an external account:

```powershell
$identity = Invoke-RestMethod -Method Post http://127.0.0.1:3000/v1/identity
spacetime login --token $identity.token
npm run db:publish
```

Treat that token as a secret. Do not put it in source control or shared logs.

On this Windows setup, the equivalent launch helpers are `.\Start-Tablecraft.ps1 -Database` and `.\Start-Tablecraft.ps1 -Publish`.

**Windows launchers.** The `Tablecraft-*.bat` files in the project folder run the usual commands without setting anything up first: they use an installed Node.js, npm and SpaceTimeDB CLI when there are any, and otherwise the portable tools in `%LOCALAPPDATA%\prototyper-tools` (the portable npm and SpaceTimeDB CLI are reached through small shims in `scripts\windows-bin`, which only the launchers put on PATH). Double-click one, or run it from a terminal; double-clicked ones keep their window open at the end so you can read the result.

| Launcher | Does |
| --- | --- |
| `Tablecraft-Dev.bat` | Starts the app (`npm run dev`) and opens it in your browser. |
| `Tablecraft-Test.bat` / `Tablecraft-Build.bat` | Runs the unit tests (`npm test`) / type-checks and builds (`npm run build`). |
| `Tablecraft-Database.bat` | Starts the local SpaceTimeDB server on `127.0.0.1:3000`, with its data in `%LOCALAPPDATA%\tablecraft-spacetimedb`. Leave it running. |
| `Tablecraft-Publish.bat [database]` | Publishes the server module to that server, keeping existing data (`tablecraft-studio` when no name is given). |
| `Tablecraft-Npm.bat <arguments>` | Runs any other npm command, for example `Tablecraft-Npm.bat run db:generate` or `Tablecraft-Npm.bat run cli -- --help`. |
| `Tablecraft-Spacetime.bat <arguments>` | Runs any SpaceTimeDB CLI command, for example `Tablecraft-Spacetime.bat login show` (who the CLI publishes as) or `Tablecraft-Spacetime.bat list --server http://127.0.0.1:3000` (the databases that identity owns). |

If publishing says the CLI "is not authorized to perform action on database", that database was first published under another SpaceTimeDB identity: sign in as that identity (`Tablecraft-Spacetime.bat login`), or publish under a new name (`Tablecraft-Publish.bat tablecraft-dev`) and enter that database name when you host or join.

In the app, choose **Invite players**, enter a room code, then **Host this table**; you play under the name from **Settings**. In another browser profile/device, choose **Join room** with the same server, database (`tablecraft-studio`) and code. Guests can also join straight from Home with **Join a table**; the room opens as a project in their workshop, and a local copy stays there after they leave. Hosting turns the table's hotseat seats into empty seats; the host keeps the seat they were playing. Joining never adds a seat: guests first choose **Pick your seat** (take an empty seat, which is renamed after them, take a new seat (also named after them), or **Watch as viewer**). Viewers can select and inspect everything, use the camera and edit component designs, but cannot move, draw, spawn, undo or change table settings. In **Players**, click any empty seat to switch to it, or use **Leave seat and watch** / **Take a new seat**. A seat is freed for others when its player leaves or their last connection closes. Separate browser profiles provide distinct identities; tabs in the same profile share an identity. A browser identity has one active live room at a time.

For other computers, use a reachable SpaceTimeDB server and a reachable frontend. The default development listeners are deliberately loopback-only. For example, start Vite with `npm run dev -- --host 0.0.0.0`, bind SpaceTimeDB to a LAN interface, allow the chosen ports through your firewall, and enter that machine's LAN address in the connection dialog. Use HTTPS/WSS behind a reverse proxy for internet deployment.

Optional frontend defaults in `.env.local`:

```dotenv
VITE_SPACETIMEDB_URI=https://your-spacetimedb-host
VITE_SPACETIMEDB_DATABASE=tablecraft-studio
```

After server schema changes, run `npm run db:generate`, then republish. Shared command changes also require republishing the module, even when the schema is unchanged. Generated client bindings are included.

### Hosting on GitHub Pages

The frontend is a static site. [.github/workflows/pages.yml](.github/workflows/pages.yml) runs the unit tests, builds with `BASE_PATH=/<repository>/` and deploys `dist/` to GitHub Pages on every push to `main` (or manually from the Actions tab). Enable it once under **Settings → Pages → Source: GitHub Actions**. Offline design and play work without a server; saves stay in each visitor's browser.

Pages cannot run SpaceTimeDB, so the deployed build defaults multiplayer to Maincloud (`https://maincloud.spacetimedb.com`, database `tablecraft-xemar5`). Override either with the repository variables `SPACETIMEDB_URI` and `SPACETIMEDB_DATABASE`. Pages is served over HTTPS, so any other server must offer HTTPS/WSS. Both values are public in the built site.

Publish the module to Maincloud with `spacetime login` (browser sign-in to the account that owns the database) and `npm run db:publish:maincloud`. Republish there after server or shared command changes, just as for a local server; pushing to `main` only redeploys the frontend.

### Trust model

Rooms are collaborative prototyping spaces for **trusted playtesters**, not adversarial competitive games:

- Room tables are private. A sender-specific view exposes the room joined by that identity.
- Knowing the room code grants membership and collaborative editing access.
- Server reducers validate commands and serialize mutations. Each identity occupies at most one seat; seat changes and ordinary hand actions are restricted to the sender's own seat (any seated player may also edit or remove empty seats). Viewers without a seat may only edit designs. The identity that created the room is its **host**: the host may also change any seat's settings (name, color, position, rotation, hand toggles) and remove other players' seats, but cannot take over their ownership or act from their hands. These host overrides are marked in red in the seat inspector, hand toolbar and **Players** list. A player whose seat the host removes becomes a viewer and can take another seat. Rooms created before host support have no host. The optional visible-hand transfer setting explicitly lets any room member move cards into or out of any hand.
- Your live actions (moving, drawing, spawning, flipping, rolling, shuffling, editing seats or designs, and taking a seat) show immediately while the room confirms them, instead of waiting for the server. If the server rejects an action, the table snaps back to the authoritative state. Undo waits for the server because the undo history is kept there.
- **Hidden hands and pieces hidden from seats are visual privacy, not server-side secrecy.** Every room member receives the entire project state, including CSV data, stack order and hand assignments. Developer tools can inspect that information.
- Component designs use last-write-wins replacement. Two simultaneous edits to the same design are not merged. Scripts likewise: saving replaces the whole script.
- **Scripts run on the server and in every browser.** They are sandboxed Lua with no file, network, clock or `load` access and a per-run instruction limit, but within a room they can do anything a player can do to the table and memory. Only the host changes scripts unless the host lets players do it; a room's scripts come with the project that was hosted, so only host projects whose scripts you trust. `update(dt)` runs on a server timer while anyone in the room is connected; each change it makes sends the room to every player, so keep updates light and their interval reasonable.
- Seats and their hands stay on the table when players leave; returning players (or anyone else) can take the empty seat again. There is no account-based cross-device identity recovery.

## Designing components

- Start from a type in **Add component**: cards, tokens, boards, dice, books, artwork, buttons, imported textures or fonts, and (last) scripts.
- Add text, vector shapes, images, linked components and nested groups. The collapsible hierarchy supports drag-and-drop reordering, reparenting, and moving layers back to the component root. Select layers there like in a file list: click selects one layer, Ctrl/Cmd-click adds or removes one, Shift-click selects every visible layer from the last clicked one (add Ctrl/Cmd to keep the current selection), and Up/Down on a focused layer move the selection (Shift extends it). Left/Right still nudge the selected layers.
- Hover a layer row (or reach it with the keyboard) to hide it with the eye control, which takes no room in the row otherwise; hidden layers keep their control visible so they can be shown again. The selected layer's eye toggle in **Design** does the same. Hiding a group hides its subtree without deleting it or changing its layout properties; flow layouts reflow around hidden children. Visibility is saved with the design, applies to previews and play, and combines with CSV visibility conditions.
- Use **Add shape** to choose and immediately insert a rectangle, ellipse, regular polygon or star. **Create group** (folder icon) groups the selected layers, or adds an empty transparent group when nothing is selected. Edit fill, stroke and stroke weight in the inspector.
- The **Pen tool** (pen icon, or **P**) draws a path: click to add straight corners, drag to pull out curve handles, click the first point to close the shape, or click the last point again, press **Enter** or **Esc**, or pick the **Select tool** (**V**) to finish an open path. Backspace removes the last point while drawing. Paths use the canvas coordinates you draw them at, and their layer box fits the points.
- **Convert to path** (the curve icon next to the boolean buttons, or **Ctrl/Cmd+E**) turns selected shapes and boolean groups into editable pen paths. Rectangles (including rounded corners), ellipses, polygons and stars keep their exact outline as points and Bézier handles. A boolean group becomes one path; when its result has holes or separate pieces it becomes a group of outline paths combined with **Exclude**, which keeps the holes. Boolean results are polygons, so curves in them become many straight points (simplified to the 128-point limit).
- On a selected pen path, click a segment to split its curve exactly; Alt-click an anchor to delete it, double-click to toggle its handles, and Shift-drag a handle to mirror its opposite. Delete/Backspace removes the selected anchor (minimum three on closed paths, two on open paths). Path bounds account for curve extrema and stroke; flow-layout children retain their allocated layout space. Ancestor frames and component edges still clip their contents.
- Wildwood's botanical illustrations are one linked artwork component made of editable vector layers, not a shape fill. The card, token and board templates in **Add component** draw the same art in (in an **Art** frame, the same for every variant), so adding one adds no other component. Older saved botanical shapes are upgraded to linked artwork on load/import or when joining a room; their original shape and text are retained. Upgrades that would exceed project limits are rejected rather than silently discarding data.
- Select two or more vector layers and click **Union**, **Subtract**, **Intersect** or **Exclude (XOR)** under the layer name in the **Design** tab to create a live compound. These buttons appear when only shapes or boolean groups are selected and need at least two. The source shapes stay in the layer hierarchy and update the boolean result when edited; change the operation or **Uncombine** to recover the editable sources. Compounds support holes and overlapping regions. Bézier curves are sampled for boolean calculations; the original paths remain editable and are not flattened in the project.
- **Link component…** nests a live reference to another project component. Linked designs refresh in the Designer, component previews, playground, hands and exports. Nested data can inherit the parent row, use a selected row from the linked component, or read a row index from a parent CSV field. Parent-field row indices are zero-based integers; a missing, malformed or out-of-range value displays a visible error instead of silently choosing a row. Component export includes its nested dependency closure; imports remap linked IDs. A linked component cannot be deleted until its parent links are removed or changed.
- **Nested fitting** controls how a linked component fills its layer: **Stretch** fills both dimensions independently (the default for existing saves); **Fit inside** preserves aspect ratio with space around the artwork; **Fill / crop (envelope)** preserves aspect ratio and crops overflow. Aspect-preserving modes are centered and adapt to the layer's actual layout size.
- Select multiple layers in the hierarchy with Ctrl/Cmd-click (or Shift-click). Drag the selection on the canvas, edit shared properties, group it under a transparent parent, or delete it together. Designer edits support undo and redo. A continuous edit is one undo step, recorded when you finish it: typing in a field (until it loses focus or you press Enter), dragging a slider, color picker or number scrubber (until release), or holding an arrow key to nudge.
- Designer shortcuts (outside fields and dialogs): Delete/Backspace deletes the selection; Ctrl/Cmd+X, C, V and D cut, copy, paste and duplicate layer subtrees; Ctrl/Cmd+G groups selected layers, or ungroups when only groups are selected. Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+G also ungroups (or uncombines selected compounds). Ctrl/Cmd+Z and Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+Z (or Ctrl+Y) undo and redo; Ctrl/Cmd+A selects root layers, Escape clears selection, and arrows nudge by 1 unit (Shift: 10). Ctrl/Cmd+] brings the selected layers forward and Ctrl/Cmd+[ sends them backward one step among their siblings; with Shift they go to the front or back (the **Bring forward** and **Send backward** buttons in the **Position and size** heading do the same). The layers list shows paint order: the first layer is at the back. The in-editor clipboard stays on the original component face and retains the original parent. Grouping requires consecutive siblings in fixed-size absolute layouts; ungrouping also requires a transparent, fully opaque frame. Groups never clip their content, so children may extend beyond a group's bounds in the designer, on the table and in previews. Unsupported operations report an error rather than changing placement unexpectedly.
- When a single fixed-size layer is selected, drag the handles on its canvas outline to resize from an edge or corner (the opposite side stays put; Shift keeps proportions on corners, and pen paths scale their points) or drag the round handle above it to rotate about the layer's pivot (Shift snaps to 15°). The blue ring with a cross marks the pivot: drag it to move the pivot (it snaps to the corners, edge middles and centre) without moving the layer. Each drag is one undo step. Layers inside row/column stacks or with hug/fill sizing are edited in the inspector instead (hug layers in an absolute parent can still be dragged to move them).
- Use the mouse wheel over the Designer canvas to zoom around the pointer (5% to 1000%); drag with the middle mouse button or Space+left mouse button to pan. Moving and resizing snap to about one screen pixel, so they are smooth at any zoom: whole units at 100% and below, finer steps (down to 0.01) when zoomed in. Path anchors, handles, the selection outline and the hover outline keep the same on-screen size at every zoom.
- The **Design** tab groups a layer's settings like most design tools: name and actions, **Position and size**, then the layer's own settings (**Layout** for frames, **Text** and **Typography**, **Image**, **Shape** or **Pen path**, **Boolean group**, **Linked component**), then **Fill**, **Stroke**, **Effects** and **Conditional visibility**. Hover or focus the ⓘ icons for explanations.
- Text layers offer font, size (or **Auto font size**: the text shrinks to fit its box, from a **Max font size** down to a **Min font size**, measured again when the box, text or font changes; text that still doesn't fit at the minimum overflows), weight, line height, letter spacing, italic, underline, strikethrough, case (as typed, uppercase, lowercase, capitalized), horizontal alignment (left, center, right, justify), vertical alignment within the box, text color, a background fill, and **Effects**: an outline drawn around each letter and a drop shadow. Bold and italic need the font to provide them; otherwise the browser approximates them.
- Drag a component from the library onto the designer canvas to add it where you drop it: a texture becomes an image layer, a font a text layer set in it, and any other component a linked component (not the component itself, or one that already shows it).
- Components that can't be flipped still have a **Back** to design; the designer notes that it's only used when printing.
- **Add linked component** inserts an empty linked component layer; choose the component it shows under **Linked component** in the Design panel, where the list can be searched. Components that already show the current one are left out, so links can't loop.
- Frames support absolute positioning or horizontal/vertical stacks with wrapping, gaps, alignment and separate padding on each edge. Sizing works per axis like auto layout in other design tools, chosen with the **Fixed**, **Hug** and **Fill** buttons under **Width sizing** and **Height sizing**. **Fixed** uses the Width (or Height) field. **Hug contents** fits the layer to what it contains, so it works only for text and stack frames (Hug saved on other layers counts as Fixed). **Fill parent** stretches the layer to its parent. A sizing a layer can't use is grayed out, and hovering it says why: Hug on shapes, images, linked components and absolute frames, on text with Auto font size (which needs a set box), and on a stack whose child fills its direction; Fill in a stack that hugs its direction (there's no free space to fill); and anything but Fixed for shapes inside a boolean group. Width and Height are editable only for Fixed; for Hug and Fill they show the measured size, and switching back to Fixed keeps that size. In an absolute parent (or at the top of a face), X and Y place Fixed and Hug layers; a Fill axis has no X/Y and stretches between its two **margins** instead (left and right, or top and bottom), so only those margins are enabled. Inside a horizontal or vertical stack, the stack places its children, so X and Y are disabled, and all four margins add space around the layer; Fill shares the stack's free space along its direction and stretches across it. Disabled fields explain why on hover.
- Every layer has a **pivot**, like a pivot in game engines (Unity's RectTransform) or the reference point in design tools: a point in its box, picked from the 3×3 **Pivot** grid (corners, edge middles, centre) or typed as **Pivot X/Y** (0 to 1 across and down the layer). X and Y place the pivot, the layer turns about it, and a layer whose size changes grows around it: a Hug text pivoted at its right edge grows to the left, one pivoted at its centre grows both ways, and editing Width or Height keeps the pivot where it is. On a Fill axis or inside a stack, the layout places the box and the pivot only sets the point it turns about. Changing the pivot keeps the layer where it is (X/Y change instead). New layers are pivoted at their top-left corner, so X/Y are their top-left corner as before; layers saved turned before pivots existed keep turning about their centre, with a centre pivot. Grouping, ungrouping, boolean groups, moving a layer into another frame and the canvas handles all follow the pivot.
- Drop a layer onto the middle of a frame/group in the hierarchy to nest it; drop near an edge to reorder. To unparent a layer, drop it near an edge of a root-level layer. Drops on non-container layers reorder rather than parent. Flow-layout children are positioned by their parent instead of being freely draggable.
- Pick a font from the **Google font / binding** chooser, which lists the suggested families in their own typefaces and filters as you type; any other Google Font family name, an imported font's name or a binding such as `{{font}}` can be typed and committed with Enter. Image layers take an HTTP(S) URL, a raster base64 data URL, an imported texture's name or a binding. Fonts and remote textures require internet access; external image hosts must permit embedding.
- **Assets by name:** a bound value that matches an imported texture's (or font's) name selects that asset, so a CSV column `art` with values `goblin`/`orc` and textures named `goblin` and `orc` gives each row its own picture through `{{art}}`. Writing an asset name as the binding itself, as in `{{goblin}}`, picks it directly when no CSV column has that name. Names match trimmed and case-insensitively. Asset names are unique across textures and fonts: imports add a number to repeated names (`wood`, `wood 2`), and renaming an asset to a used name is refused. Component exports include assets their layers pick by name for their own rows.
- **Picture fit and placement:** an image layer's **Picture fit** is **Fill and crop** (the default), **Fit inside**, **Stretch**, **Original size** or **Tile**. Double-click an image layer on the canvas (or choose **Edit picture**) to move, rotate and resize the picture inside the layer, which crops it to its bounds and corner radius: drag the picture to move it, drag corners to scale proportionally (Shift stretches), edges to stretch one side, and the round handle to rotate (Shift snaps to 15°). Press Enter or Escape, or click elsewhere, to finish. The placement is also editable as **Picture X/Y**, **Scale X/Y %** and **Picture rotation**, and **Reset** restores the fitted picture. Canvas editing needs a fixed-size layer in an absolute parent; hug-sized image layers keep the original fill-and-crop picture.
- Open **Data bindings** to paste or import CSV. A header such as `name,cost,image` exposes `{{name}}`, `{{cost}}` and `{{image}}` in text/image layers. Mix static text and multiple bindings, e.g. `Power: {{power}}`. A layer can optionally be shown only when a CSV field equals a value, or (with **Condition reads → A value in memory** in its **Conditional visibility**) when a value in the scripts' memory does, such as `round` or `score.red` equals `3` (true and false read as `true` and `false`): memory conditions follow memory as it changes, on every piece and for every player. In Wildwood, the Amber tally's **Supply empty** note shows while `memory.supplyEmpty` is true.
- CSV editing supports single-column data (including dice values), commas, tabs, semicolons and quoted multiline text. Every column needs a unique non-empty header; data rows must have the same number of cells. Empty CSV clears the data source. Invalid data is reported without replacing the existing rows.
- Each CSV row defines a variant. Use the row selector in the designer's top toolbar for any component type, including artwork; it stays synced with **Preview row** in **Data bindings** and the dice variant editor. All three selectors label variants as `N. value`, using the one-based row number and the first CSV column's value. Like every list filled from your data (pages, dice variants, imported fonts and textures, pen path points and a linked component's preview row), they open with a search box: type to filter, use the arrow keys and press Enter to pick, or Escape to close. The toolbar scrolls horizontally with the mouse wheel or trackpad, with edge shadows indicating more controls. Components without data show a disabled **Default** option. **Spawn all variants** creates one instance of each. CSV updates immediately refresh every existing instance, including held and stacked pieces, on all connected clients.
- Color buttons show the current color across the whole button (over a checkerboard when it is translucent). Color controls include an **Alpha** slider (0% transparent to 100% opaque) for fills, text, strokes, component backgrounds and player colors (not the profile's default color). Color entry accepts CSS colors including `#RRGGBBAA`, `rgba(...)` and `transparent`; choosing a new RGB color preserves its alpha. Color alpha is separate from layer opacity.
- Library previews show a variant-count badge and additional variant faces for any component type with multiple data rows, not just cards. The type filters scroll horizontally with the mouse wheel or trackpad when the library is narrow; edge shadows indicate more filters, without a visible scrollbar.
- Instances reference **row indices**, not copied content. Reordering CSV rows changes which data those instances display. Removed rows resolve bindings to empty strings; existing pieces are not silently deleted.
- **Behavior** controls flippability/back design, hands, front visibility, stacking, shuffling, stack tags, **Preview when locked** (show the hover preview even while a piece is locked) and the **Rotation step** for Q/E and the rotate buttons. **Stack tags** sit right under **Can be stacked**: a list of tags (type a new one and press Enter, or pick an existing one from the arrow beside the field, whose list has a search box; × removes it). Tag filters pick existing tags the same way. Stackable pieces that share at least one stack tag can stack, and pieces without tags stack with anything stackable; turning **Can be stacked** off keeps a component out of stacks altogether.

**Stack filters.** Every stack has a filter that decides which pieces may join it. A new stack (Stack selected pieces, G, or dropping pieces onto a piece) takes every stack tag its pieces share, so a stack of `red blue` and `red blue gold` pieces takes pieces tagged red or blue; pieces with no tag in common can't be stacked. Select a stack (or an empty persistent stack) to see and edit its filter in the Selection tab, as plain rules instead of a formula: each rule lists tags a piece must **have** and tags it must **not** have, and a piece fits when it matches any rule (**Or pieces with…** adds another rule). For example "(blue and red) or not white" is one rule with blue and red under Has, and a second rule with white under But not. A filter without rules takes any piece, and pieces without tags always fit. Stacks dropped onto a stack keep the filter of the stack they land on; filters only decide who joins, so pieces already in a stack stay when its filter changes.
- **Component type** in Behavior switches between card, token, board, dice, book and artwork without resetting artwork, dimensions, data or behavior settings. Dice gain rolling controls; tokens use round component rendering.
- **Books** (**Add component → Books**) are one piece whose CSV rows are its pages, in order. On the table a book looks like a stack and shows its page (for example `2 / 5`), but nothing can be drawn or taken out of it. Hover or select it and press **Shift+Q** / **Shift+E** to turn back or forward (stopping at the first and last page), or use the page buttons in the Selection inspector. Edit pages under **Book pages** in Behavior, like dice variants; removing a page remaps placed books the same way.
- Dice **variants** are their CSV rows, with equal probability on each roll. In Behavior, choose a variant, edit its column values, or add/remove a variant; Data bindings remains available for adding columns and bulk editing. At least one variant is retained by the variant editor. Removal shifts later placed dice and fixed nested-row references down; references to the deleted row use the next row or the last remaining row. Parent-field row bindings keep their CSV values and report invalid rows as usual. These changes update all instances and are included in design undo/redo; undo restores the design data, not previous dice roll results.
- Turning off hand/stack/flip support normalizes existing instances. Disabling player hands at the table level only hides the hand UI; it does not discard held pieces.
- Export a component ZIP from **Behavior**, including its nested components, textures and fonts. Import projects from Home and component bundles through the library's **Import component ZIP** button; component imports also work inside live sessions. Portable JSON imports are no longer supported; existing browser-local workspaces and snapshots remain readable.
- Choose the **Textures** or **Fonts** tile in **Add component** to import PNG, JPEG, WebP or GIF images, or TTF, OTF, WOFF or WOFF2 fonts (each tile only accepts its own kind). Each becomes a **Texture** or **Font** library component, not a spawnable piece. Use **Imported texture** on image layers and **Imported font** on text layers. Deleting the asset component deletes its embedded file, clears image references, and resets text references to DM Sans. Asset files are saved in the ZIP and work offline, including linked component bundles and live rooms. The Google font chooser also includes serif, sans-serif, display, handwriting and monospace families; these need internet access.
- Deleting a component requires confirmation and removes all its spawned instances; referenced nested components must first be unlinked.

## Playing

The compact top bar includes project, mode, save and session controls.

**Settings** (the round profile button in the top bar, also on Home) holds per-browser preferences: your player profile (name and default color), the theme (**System**, **Light** or **Night**), the **Interface scale** (50–200%, default 100%, with presets from 75% to 150%) that makes the whole interface larger or smaller, and the default SpaceTimeDB server and database used to prefill **Invite players** and **Join a table**. Settings save automatically to local storage and are never shared with rooms or projects. The first time you open Tablecraft, Settings opens as a welcome screen with a randomly chosen opaque default color, and it cannot be closed until you enter a name. Names are required: leading and trailing spaces are trimmed, a whitespace-only name is rejected, and names are limited to 32 printable ASCII characters (other characters are dropped as you type). Settings is the only place to change your name: the session form shows who you are playing as, and in a live room a claimed seat's name comes from its player's profile and can't be edited in the seat inspector (offline hotseat seat names stay editable). The profile name and default color are used when hosting or joining a room and for your first hotseat seat; the default color has no alpha. The theme previews use the colors each theme actually renders. Night mode restyles the whole interface in muted grays; designed component artwork keeps its own colors. Its panel icons independently toggle the component library (books icon), layers/pieces panel, hand tray and table inspector. Panel visibility and camera changes are local to the browser; toggling a panel preserves the camera. The app always fits the browser window, without page scrolling, even on short screens or with Windows display scaling; panels scroll internally instead.

The header's **Actions** menu (☰) holds everything else: **Save**, **Load** and **Print to PDF** first, then **Undo** and **Redo** (in the playground, with their shortcuts), the **Visibility** and **Scripting** submenus, and **Close project**. A submenu opens beside its item when you point at it, click it or press the right arrow (on whichever side has room); the left arrow or Escape goes back. **Visibility** shows or hides the panels: **Component library** (in both the playground and the component designer, kept when switching modes), **Layers panel** (designer) or **Pieces panel** (playground) to the right of the library, **Hand panel** and **Table inspector** (playground) and **Designer inspector** (the designer's right panel, shown and hidden separately from the table inspector). Items toggle without closing the menu; a click elsewhere, Escape or Tab closes it, and the arrow keys move through it. **Scripting** opens the **Scripts** and **Memory** windows. Below the components, under **All**, the library lists the project's **Scripts** (with their size and how many functions they define); the library search finds them too, and clicking one opens it in the Scripts window. **Add component** also offers **Scripts**, which adds a script and opens it. The library header has **Link from another project** (link icon) and **Add component** (+) buttons. In the playground, hover a library preview and use its + badge to spawn the component; the badge is hidden in the designer. Drag the separators to resize the library, layers/pieces panel, designer inspector, table inspector, and hand tray. Separators have a thin 2px visible line within a 6px drag target. Focus a separator and use the arrow keys (Shift for larger steps), Home or End to resize without a mouse. Preferred panel sizes are saved locally, not in the project or shared room, and may be temporarily constrained on smaller screens. The active inspector tabs are retained while switching components or modes during the session.

When undo history is cleared (for example after a design change or a seat change), a toast near the bottom of the table explains why, in the same style as the **Pieces can't be stacked** message. It hides after a few seconds or when clicked, and each new reason appears once; the Undo button tooltip keeps the reason. Dismissing it does not change shared history. Notification banners have an **X** button to dismiss them locally; dismissing a storage or snapshot error does not enable writes or overwrite unreadable saves. Reload to show an unresolved warning again.

The playground **Pieces** panel lists every piece on the table, topmost first; stacks appear as one row with their size and can be expanded to list their pieces. Pieces held in hands are not listed. Click a row to select it (a stack row selects the whole stack, a piece row only that piece), Ctrl/Cmd-click to add or remove a row, and Shift-click to select a range; the selection shows in the **Selection** tab. Locked pieces show a lock icon and can be selected from the list. The row for the current selection is highlighted and scrolled into view. A group's pieces always sit together in table order, in the same order the panel lists them: clicking a grouped piece on the table brings its whole group (with any groups around it) to the front, with the clicked piece on top inside it, and grouping scattered pieces gathers them at the topmost one. Selecting a locked piece, or a piece in a locked group, leaves the table order alone. The lock button on a group row (it and the order-lock button show when you hover or focus the row, and stay while they're on) locks the **group** itself: its pieces and subgroups can't be moved, rotated, flipped or drawn while it is locked, but each piece keeps its own lock, so unlocking the group frees exactly the pieces that weren't locked on their own (the Selection tab says when a piece is held by its group).

**Order locks** are a second kind of lock, for a piece's place in the hierarchy rather than on the table: an order-locked piece can still be moved, turned and flipped, but it keeps its place in the Pieces panel and the table order. It isn't raised when you pick it up, **Bring forward**, **Send backward** and Ctrl/Cmd+]/[ refuse to move it, its row can't be dragged, and it can't be moved into or out of a group (or grouped). Other pieces still move above and below it. Lock a piece's order with **Lock order** in the Selection tab; a group's with the list button on its row (next to its lock) or **Lock order** in the group's Selection tab, which keeps the group, its subgroups and all their pieces where they are (Move up, Move down, Out of group and Ungroup say why they're unavailable). Rows whose order is locked show a list icon.

Pieces can be organised into named, nestable **groups** in the Pieces panel. Select pieces (or groups), then point at the Pieces panel and press **G**, or use the group button in its header, to group them; a group containing every selected piece of whole groups nests those groups inside the new one. **Shift+G** (or the ungroup button) ungroups the selected groups, moving their contents up a level. The pointer must be over the Pieces panel for these shortcuts; elsewhere **G** still stacks. Click a group to select the group itself, not its pieces (Ctrl/Cmd-click and Shift-click mix groups and pieces). Grouping selects the new group, and ungrouping selects its pieces. The Selection tab for a group renames it, shows its piece count and parent group, and has **Select pieces** (every piece in it and its subgroups), **Move up** / **Move down** (past its neighbour, like Alt+Up/Down), **Out of group** (up a level), **Lock** and **Ungroup**. Double-click a group or press F2 to rename it, use its arrow to collapse it, and drag rows onto the middle of a group to move them in, or onto the empty list area to move them to the top level. The list shows render order, topmost first: drag a row onto the top or bottom edge of another row to place it just above or below that row (and in the same group), or focus a row and press **Alt+Up**/**Alt+Down** to move it past its neighbour. A group (with its subgroups) moves as one block, so its pieces stay together in render order; dragging a selected row moves the whole selection. Boards always stay underneath. Groups are saved and shared with the table and included in tabletop undo; a stack always shares one group (stacking adopts the bottom piece's group), pieces taken into a hand leave their group, and groups left empty disappear. Groups never move pieces on the table; rearranging rows only changes which pieces are drawn on top.

The Playground inspector opens on **Selection**, with settings and actions for selected pieces, stacks or hand cards. Its **Scripting** section at the bottom (closed until you open it, then kept open as you select other pieces) shows the **Piece id** (or how many pieces are selected) and, for a stack, the **Stack id**, each with a copy button, for scripts and the CLI. A Selection button that can't be used right now looks disabled but says why: hover or focus it to see the reason, or click it to get it as a notification (for example "Spirit die can't be held in a hand"). Clicking a seat on the table selects it instead of switching to it: the Selection tab then shows that seat's name, color (with alpha), position and rotation, plus the same hand actions as the hand toolbar and a **Switch to this seat** button. Every hand toggle (face up on entry, table-hand visibility, insert/take permissions and the seat lock) is that seat's own setting, saved with the playground. Only the seat's active player can change it, so these toggles, along with shuffle and reverse, are disabled for other seats. You can only edit seats you own. Press Escape or click empty table space to deselect. When nothing is selected, it prompts you to select a component. **Table** contains shared table settings, while **Players** manages seats. Switching tabs does not change the selection.

Hover the horizontal overflow shadows in the library filters or designer toolbar to reveal scroll arrows. Hold an arrow to scroll continuously; release to stop. Focus an arrow and press Enter or Space to scroll a step.

The library is a grid that fits as many columns as the panel is wide. The buttons beside **Project library** switch between three views, remembered in this browser: **Large grid** (previews with names), **Small grid** (tiles a third of the size, art only; the name is in the tooltip, and the pencil that appears in a hovered tile's top right corner edits it) and **List** (name and details on the left, a small icon on the right; hovering a name shows the edit hint, as in the large grid). Scripts are library items too, after the components: click one to open it in the Scripts window, and use the **Scripts** filter to list only them.

Drag a component's or script's grip to reorder the library in any view (in the small grid it shows when you hover a tile); the insertion line shows whether it will move before or after the target, beside a tile in the grids or above or below a row in the list. Focus the grip and use the arrow keys for keyboard reordering. Components and scripts each reorder among themselves. Filters and search retain the saved project order; moving a filtered item repositions only that item relative to the drop target. Library order is saved, exported and shared in multiplayer, and does not change placed pieces or consume tabletop undo history. Agents can use `{"type":"reorderComponent","id":"COMPONENT_ID","targetId":"TARGET_ID","position":"before"}` (or `"after"`).

Click a library preview to spawn its first variant; hovering it shows a drag hint. Click a component's name to open it in the designer; hovering the name shows a pen and an edit hint. For components with multiple CSV rows, the preview also has a **Spawn all variants** button beside **+**, and dragging the preview onto the table spawns every variant at the drop point. Both place the variants in a single stack when the component is stackable, or spread them out otherwise. Use the arrow at the right of the component's name row to show or hide its variant previews. Click a variant to select it, Ctrl/Cmd-click to toggle variants, and Shift-click to select a range (Ctrl/Cmd+Shift adds the range). Drag a selected variant onto the table to spawn one of each selected row; dragging an unselected variant spawns only that row. **Spawn all variants**, also shown below the expanded variants list, works the same way as the preview button. Up to 1,000 rows can be spawned at once. Each drop is one undoable action; the pieces remain linked to their component and rows. Dice rows are faces of one die rather than variants, and book rows are its pages: a die or book has no Variants list or spawn-all button, and clicking or dragging it always spawns one piece showing its first face or page. Hover or select dice and press **Shift+Q** / **Shift+E** to step to the previous or next face (wrapping around).

Drag pieces to move; Shift/Ctrl/Cmd-click toggles pieces in the selection, or drag an empty area to box-select (Shift adds to the selection). Locked pieces ignore plain clicks, so a box selection can start over them (for example on a locked board) and never includes them; Ctrl/Cmd-click a locked piece to select it, or hover it and press L to unlock it. Shift-drag the top of a stack to move just that piece. Clicking a piece brings it to the front. Ctrl/Cmd+] brings the hovered piece (or the selection) forward past the next piece it overlaps and Ctrl/Cmd+[ sends it backward under the one below; with Shift it goes to the front or back of the table. Stacks move as a whole and boards always stay underneath. **Bring forward** and **Send backward** in the selection's actions do the same. The selection ring, its corner handles and the focus and stacking highlights keep the same on-screen size at any zoom or piece scale. Pointing at an unlocked piece shows a light hover outline. Shadows, the hover, selection and stacking outlines, hand selections and the edges of a stack follow each piece's drawn shape: a component with a transparent background is not rounded to its kind's usual card or token shape, so a hex tile gets a hex outline (keyboard focus still shows a box). The table and the fullscreen preview are redrawn sharp at the current zoom a moment after you stop zooming or panning. Move, rotate, flip, scale, clone, lock, delete or draw the whole selection together. Rotating with Q/E and the rotate buttons preserves relative differences; the inspector's **Rotation** and **Scale** fields set the same value on every selected piece and show **–** while the pieces differ; flipping a whole stack reverses its displayed order and faces without rewriting the underlying members. Drawing or extracting from it takes the visible top face. With a whole stack selected, **Draw one** takes its top piece into your hand (the remaining stack stays selected) and **Draw all** takes every piece. With two or more pieces or stacks selected, each shows its selection order and a small stack glyph marking where it will land: **Stack selected pieces** (or **G**) puts the first selected at the bottom and the last on top (a box selection numbers pieces in the order the box reaches them). If the selection cannot be stacked, the glyphs are crossed out and a **Pieces can't be stacked** message explains why. Drop compatible unlocked pieces or complete stacks onto another piece or stack to combine them, with the dragged pieces on top. A highlighted target and drop hint preview stacking; **hold Alt** to move without stacking, including when playing from hand. Dragged pieces stay above the hand tray. Middle-drag or the hand tool pans; scroll zooms (up to 300%); the camera buttons rotate the view; **Fit table** resets the view and camera. Preview starts at 100%; use the wheel to zoom, move the pointer to pan, **F** to flip when permitted, and **Q/E** to rotate. When the piece is larger than the space between the title and help bars, the pointer position picks the part you see: the pointer at an edge of the window shows that edge of the piece, and the view follows the pointer as you zoom or rotate too. A stack's preview shows how many pieces it holds next to its name. **Grids.** A table has any number of grid layers: **square** grids (cell width and height, linked into square cells until you turn **Link width and height** off), **hex** grids (pointy tops in rows or flat tops in columns; the size is the distance between neighbouring cell centres) and **snap points** you place by hand, each with an offset that moves its points, its own colour (with transparency, through its alpha slider; square and hex grids default to 10% alpha so they stay in the background, while snap point markers are opaque), and switches for showing it and for snapping to it. Square and hex grids have no edge: they cover the table wherever you pan. When you zoom far out, a grid fades as its cells shrink below about 14 screen pixels, and isn't drawn under 5, so it doesn't turn into a dense texture (the grid being edited stays visible). Turn on the **Grids** tool (the grid button under the select and pan tools, or **Table → Edit grids**) to manage them: the panel lists the layers (add square, hex or points grids, rename, reorder, show or hide, snap or not, delete) and edits the selected one. With the tool on, dragging the table moves the selected grid, and on a points grid clicking the table adds a snap point, dragging a point moves it, and selecting one lets you give it a label and its own rotation (Delete removes it). Each point of the edited points grid shows its **snap distance** as a dashed circle: a piece snaps to the point when its centre is dropped inside it. Pieces can't be moved while the tool is on, and Escape or **Done** turns it off. While a points grid is being edited, the **Points** panel takes the Pieces panel's place and lists its snap points in nestable **point groups** (they only organise points, not snapping): click a point to select it on the table and in the grid panel, **New point group** groups the selected point (or makes a group inside the selected group), double-click (or F2) renames a group, **Ungroup** moves its contents up a level, drag rows into groups or above and below each other (Alt+Up/Down moves the focused row), and with a group selected, new points you add go into it and its points are highlighted on the table. A grid can also **turn snapped pieces** to a fixed rotation, and a snap point can override it.

**Component grids.** A component can carry its own grids, of any kind and with all the same settings, so a board, mat or card brings its spaces with it. In the designer, grids work like layers: **Add grid** in the toolbar adds a square grid, hex grid or snap points over the whole component and selects it. The component's grids are rows above its layers in the **Layers** panel (on both faces), in the order pieces snap to them: click one to select it, drag rows to reorder them, and use a row's eye to show or hide the grid on the table. The selected grid's settings show in the inspector, in the component's own coordinates, with buttons to show or hide it, snap to it or not, and delete it, plus its **Bounds** (X, Y, width and height; **Fit component** covers the whole component). The grids apply only inside their bounds: no points outside them, and pieces snap only when dropped inside or within half a cell of them, which the inspector gives in pixels and the canvas shows as a dotted frame around the bounds. On the canvas the selected grid's bounds are a dashed frame whose handles resize them; dragging the canvas moves a square or hex grid's points, and on a points grid clicking adds a snap point and dragging moves one. A points grid also lists its points in the inspector (click one to select it, drag rows to reorder them; there are no point groups here), and shows each point's snap distance as a dashed circle. Delete removes the selected point, or with none selected the grid itself (the toolbar's delete button too), Escape deselects the point and then the grid, and each change is a design edit you can undo. On the table every piece of the component carries its grids, drawn over its face, moving, turning and scaling with it. Pieces dropped on it snap to them before the table's grids (the topmost piece's first, and never a piece to its own grids), and a snapped piece takes the piece's rotation plus the grid's or point's turn. Only the top piece of a stack carries grids, and pieces in hands carry none.

When a piece or stack is dropped, it snaps to the nearest point of the **first grid in the list** that snaps, takes the piece and has a point in reach: square and hex grids always do; snap points only within their **Snap distance** (50 px by default). So put snap points above a square grid to have pieces land on them when dropped close by and on the grid elsewhere. Which pieces a grid takes is decided like stack filters: each component lists **Grid tags** in its Behavior tab, and each grid has a **Snaps pieces** filter of rules (pieces with these tags but not those, or any of several rules); components without grid tags snap to every grid, and **Can snap to grids** turns a component's snapping off. Snapping happens on drops (from the table or a hand) and is decided by the shared engine, so every player gets the same result; each dragged piece or stack snaps on its own, stacks stay together, and a multi-piece drop is applied and undone atomically. Grid changes are table actions that undo. A piece whose centre sits exactly on a grid point counts as snapped to it, which scripts can read (`piece.snap`) and react to (`onSnap`). Projects from before grid layers keep their one square grid (from the old grid settings) until you change it. The Tidewater Isles sample snaps tiles to a hex grid under the island, huts, lighthouses and boardwalks to the cells' corners and edges (boardwalks turned along them), and tide markers and the squall to the tiles' number slots.

## Scripting

Projects can carry **Lua scripts** that buttons and events run for everyone at the table. Open them from **Scripting** in the header's **Actions** menu: **Scripts** for the scripts and **Memory** for shared memory. Both open as windows over the workspace that you can move, resize and keep open while you play; their place and size are remembered per browser.

**How scripts run.** Scripts are Lua 5.3, run by [Fengari](https://fengari.io/) (a Lua interpreter written in JavaScript), inside the same command engine as every other table action. A project's scripts load in their list order and share their globals. Every run starts fresh: it loads the scripts, calls one function and ends, so plain Lua globals don't survive between runs. Keep anything that should last in `memory`. Offline the run happens in your browser; in a live room the **server** runs it (your browser runs the same script at once to show the result early), so every player, and players who join later, see the same table and memory. Runs are deterministic: `math.random` and `game.random` draw from a seed carried by the action, and there is no clock, file, network or `load`. A run that executes more than about 5 million Lua instructions is stopped. Integers are 32-bit (larger whole numbers become floats).

**Buttons and actions.** **Create component → Buttons** makes a button component (or set any component's type to **Button** in its Behavior tab). Choose its function under **When pressed, run** in Behavior: the list shows the global functions the scripts define, such as `function nextRound(piece, player) ... end`. On the table, clicking a button piece (or focusing it and pressing Enter or Space) calls that function with the button's piece and the pressing player's seat. A press doesn't select the button; Ctrl/Cmd- or Shift-click selects it, and unlocked buttons can still be dragged. Any other component can have a **Script action** too: its selected pieces get a **Run** button in the Selection grid. Viewers can't run functions. Buttons are on-screen controls and aren't printed.

**Popups.** **Create component → Popups** makes a popup component (or set a component's type to **Popup**): a message, prompt or choice designed like any component, with `{{fields}}` from its data rows, that scripts show on players' screens with `game.popup` instead of putting it on the table (it can't be placed there, and isn't printed). Its **On screen** settings in the Behavior tab say where it shows over the table view, which is its game area: **Layout** places it at one of nine **anchors**, a **margin** in from the edges (plus an offset), and sizes it as the view changes (**Shrink to fit**, **Fill** or **Keep its size**, after its **scale**); **Fixed** puts its top-left corner at an exact X, Y at its own size. **Blur the table behind it** dims and blurs the table, which can't be used until it closes; **Players can drag it** lets each player move it on their own screen; **Players can close it** gives it a × button (and Escape closes the topmost one); **Closing** chooses whether each player closes their own copy or the first close closes it for everyone; **Close after** closes it by itself after some seconds. Its **When a layer is clicked, run** function gets `(popup, player, layer, path)`: the popup (`{ id, component, row, data, players, closedBy, player }`), the clicking seat, the clicked layer's name and the names of the layers it's in (outermost first); **Close after a click runs the action** closes it for that player afterwards, or the function can call `game.closePopup(popup.id, player)` itself. `game.popup(popup, options)` returns the open popup's id and takes `players` (only these seats see it), `row` and `data` (field values over the row's, such as `{ name = seat.name, count = 5 }`), and any of the on-screen settings for this showing (`layout`, `anchor`, `margin`, `fit`, `x`, `y`, `scale`, `blur`, `draggable`, `closable`, `dismiss`, `duration`, `closeOnAction`). `game.closePopup(popup?, players?)` closes one (by id), every open popup of a popup component, or all of them, for everyone or only some seats; `game.popups()` lists the open ones. Open popups are shared table state, kept until closed (at most 50; the oldest make room) but outside undo. While playing, clicking a popup in the library previews it on your screen only. Wildwood has four sample popups: every fifth amber gathered shows **Amber milestone** (centred over the blurred table, draggable) to the gatherer alone, and its **Share** button announces it to everyone; a wanderer reaching a stone shows **Stone reached** in the top-right corner for everyone, which closes by itself after a few seconds; reaching the **Heart of the wood** fills the blurred table with a popup in that wanderer's data row; and the **Quick rules** button opens a rules panel at a fixed spot, draggable, with a live amber count, and closes it on the next press.

**Messages for some seats.** `game.announce(text, players?)`, `game.popup` (its `players` option), `game.closePopup(popup, players?)` and `game.ping(pieces, players?)` take an optional seat or list of seats (ids, names, seat numbers or player tables): only those seats see the message, popup or pulse, and without it everyone does (an empty list reaches no one). The script console still lists every announcement, with who it was for; like hidden hands this is visual privacy for trusted playtesters, not secrecy. A hotseat screen shows what's meant for the active seat.

**The script editor.** The list on the left holds the project's scripts: **+** adds one, double-click renames, the arrows reorder, the bin deletes. The editor offers Lua highlighting, line numbers, code folding, bracket matching and closing, auto-indent, multiple cursors (Ctrl/Cmd-click, Alt-drag), find and replace (Ctrl+F), comment toggling (Ctrl+/), undo history per script, completion of the game API, events, your functions, memory keys and Lua's own library (Ctrl+Space), and syntax errors marked as you type. **Save** (Ctrl+S) shares the script with everyone; **Revert** drops unsaved edits. If someone else saves a script you're editing, a banner offers **Load theirs** or **Keep mine**. Pick a function and **Run** (Ctrl+Enter) to call it as yourself (scripts must be saved to run). **Reload scripts** runs `onUnload`, then `onLoad`. The **Console** shows `print()` output, errors (with a link to the line) and announcements from every player's actions, repeating lines counted; clear it for everyone with the eraser. **Reference** lists the API; clicking an entry inserts it. Errors from your own actions also pop up as a notification, and `game.announce` shows a notification to everyone. In a live room only the host edits scripts until the host turns on **Table → Players can edit scripts**; everyone can read them.

**Events.** Define any of these global functions and the app calls them (`player` is the seat whose action caused the event, or nil):

| Function | When |
| --- | --- |
| `onLoad()` / `onUnload()` | After the scripts are saved or reloaded / before they're replaced. Set up memory in `onLoad`. |
| `update(dt)` | Every **Script update every (s)** seconds (Table settings, 1 by default) while players are at the table; in live rooms the server's timer runs it. |
| `onMemoryChanged(changes, player)` | A player edited memory; each change has `path` (such as `"score.red"`), `keys`, `value` and `old`. |
| `onSpawn(pieces, player)` / `onDelete(pieces, player)` | Pieces were added / removed. |
| `onDraw(hand, pieces, player)` / `onPlay(hand, pieces, player)` | Pieces went into a hand / left a hand for the table. |
| `onStack(stack, pieces, player)` / `onShuffle(stack, player)` | Pieces joined a stack / a stack was shuffled. |
| `onMove`, `onRotate`, `onFlip`, `onRoll` `(pieces, player)` | Table pieces moved, turned, were turned over, or (dice) rolled. |
| `onTurnEnd(previous, next)` / `onTurnStart(player)` | The turn tracker passed the turn. |
| `onSnap(pieces, player)` | Table pieces came to rest on grid points (each piece's `snap` says which). |

Event handlers run inside the player's action. If a handler fails, its error is logged and the player's action still happens. Actions that scripts perform don't trigger events.

**The `game` API.** Pieces, stacks and seats can be passed as ids or as the tables the API returns, and most actions take one or a list. Pieces are snapshots `{ id, componentId, component, kind, row, data, x, y, rotation, scale, faceUp, locked, holder, stack, group }` (`row` counts from 1; `data` is its CSV row); read them again after changing them. Grids and points are named by id or name; points are `"col,row"` on square grids, axial `"q,r"` on hex grids and their own ids on points grids, and a piece's `snap` is `{ grid, point }` when its centre sits on one (nil otherwise), with the point's `label` on a points grid and the carrying `piece` on a grid a piece carries. Seats are `{ id, name, color, seat, isTurn }` and stacks `{ id, pieces, top, count, x, y, persistent, filter }` (`filter.anyOf` lists its rules); components include their stack `tags`.

| Reading | Acting |
| --- | --- |
| `game.pieces(filter?)` (a table such as `{ component = "Spirit cards", holder = false }`, or a function) | `game.move(pieces, x, y)`, `game.moveBy(pieces, dx, dy)` |
| `game.piece(id)`, `game.hand(player)` | `game.rotate(pieces, degrees)`, `game.setRotation(pieces, degrees)` |

Scripts follow the table's rules: locked pieces must be unlocked (`game.unlock`) before they move, and taking from or giving to hands respects each hand's permissions. A failing call (a Lua error or a refused action) changes nothing and logs its error with the script line.

**Memory.** `memory` is a table shared by scripts and players and saved with the project (offline, in ZIP saves and in live rooms, so players joining later get it). Change it like any Lua table; it's stored when the function returns. It holds text, numbers, true/false, lists and tables (no functions, no nil values, at most 512 KB of JSON). A table whose keys are exactly 1..n is a list; other tables become named values (number keys become text). The **Memory** window shows it as a tree: edit values in place, change a value's type, rename keys, add and remove values and list items, move list items up and down, search, expand or collapse everything, copy a value's Lua path or binding, **Edit as JSON**, and import or export JSON. Each edit is a shared, undoable action that runs `onMemoryChanged`. Viewers can only read it.

**Bindings.** Component layers show memory with `{{memory.score}}`, `{{memory.score.red}}` or `{{memory.players.1.name}}` (list positions from 1); `{{memory.players.#}}` is a list's length, and a list of plain values reads as its items joined with commas. Bindings work anywhere data bindings do (text, image and font names, visibility conditions), update for every player as memory changes, and a CSV column of the same name wins.

**Undo.** Everything one function call (or one action with its event handlers) does is a single undo step, memory included. Changes between `game.beginNoUndo()` and `game.endNoUndo()` stay when players undo that step; every begin needs its end before the function returns, or the call fails. `update`, `onLoad` and `onUnload` don't add undo steps.

## Saves and portability

- **Print to PDF** (in the header's **Actions** menu) lays out every copy of every piece at print size: each piece on the table, in stacks and in hands, every page of a book and every face of a die (optionally also one copy of each variant of components with no pieces). Choose the paper (A4, US Letter or A3), scale (at 100%, 96 px print one inch wide), gap, page margin and dashed cut lines. **Double-sided backs** follows each page of fronts with the backs of its pieces (a component with no back design prints its background colour; die faces and book pages have no backs), mirrored so they line up when printed on both sides and flipped on the long edge. The browser's print dialog opens; choose **Save as PDF** to get a PDF, and print at actual size. Pieces larger than the page are listed in the dialog under **Too big for one page**. With **Split large pieces** (on by default) they print at full size in several parts: every part but the last in its row and column has a hatched 10 mm glue wing, labelled with the part that is stuck onto it, and each part is labelled with its number; turned off, they shrink to fit instead.
- The workspace automatically persists in browser local storage after every accepted change. No external service is contacted for offline game-state storage.
- Component designer edits apply to the table when you return to the **Playground** (or open Home or another project). In a live room, other players see your design changes at that point too. Until then the edits are saved on their own right after each change, so closing or reloading the page mid-edit keeps them: they are applied the next time Tablecraft opens, which shows a short notice. Any other change you make while designing (adding, importing or deleting components) applies the pending designs first. Every design change is still checked as you make it.
- Projects group designs, CSV sources, pieces, seats, hands and table settings.
- **Snapshots.** A snapshot is a copy of the whole playground: designs, table, hands, seats, scripts and memory. **Save** (in the header's **Actions** menu) saves one with a name; **Load** lists the project's snapshots and sessions to load; the clock button on a Home project card shows the same list for any project. Creating or importing a project starts it with a snapshot named **<project> - initial state**. Snapshots and sessions can be renamed (the pencil beside a name) and deleted (asks once more; it can't be undone). **Load** loads a snapshot as the project's table, in place (asking first about unsaved changes); the project keeps its name and description.
- **Draft and finished projects.** A project starts as a **Draft**: each new snapshot is its latest version, and loading the project loads its latest snapshot. Mark it **Finished** at the top of **Load**, and choose its base with **Make base** (the latest draft snapshot by default). Loading a finished project then asks whether to **Start a new game**, which asks what to call the game and copies the base into a new session of that name whose first snapshot is **Start** (**Start a new game** in Load's **Sessions** asks too), or to **Continue** an existing session from its latest snapshot. While playing a session (the header shows **Session · name**), saving adds a snapshot to that session rather than to the drafts. To change the base, switch the project back to **Draft**, save new draft snapshots, then mark it finished again with the new base (or use **Make base** while finished). Sessions that started from a different snapshot than the current base are marked **Outdated** and say which snapshot they started from; they can still be continued. A finished project's base can't be deleted, nor a session's only snapshot. While a project isn't loaded, Home shows it as of its latest draft (or its base, when finished), which is also what other projects' links read.
- Snapshots are kept in this browser beside the workspace, up to 100 draft snapshots per project, 50 sessions and 100 snapshots per session. Snapshots saved by earlier versions (in one list shared by every project) are moved into the projects they were saved from the first time this version starts; any from a project that no longer exists become projects of their own.
- **Export** (the download button on a Home project card, or **Export…** in Load) saves one project as `.tablecraft.zip`, always with its current table, plus the snapshots you choose: **Base snapshot** (or **Latest draft** for a draft), **Draft history**, **This session** (the session being played, with the draft snapshot it started from), **Everything**, or **Project only**. Presets fill in the checkboxes of the draft snapshots and of each session, which you can adjust to export any selection (for example a session together with the draft history). Without its base, a finished project is imported as a draft. **Export all projects** on Home backs up the whole workspace with every snapshot and session. Importing adds the projects with their snapshots and sessions (with new identifiers); a project imported without draft snapshots starts with one of its table. Imported projects aren't loaded: load them from Home. Each ZIP contains a root `project.json` (versioned `format: "tablecraft", version: 1`, with exported snapshots and sessions under `histories`, keyed by project id), imported binary files under `assets/`, each script as a `.lua` file under `scripts/` (numbered in their order, such as `scripts/1-Rules.lua`) and script memory as `memory.json` (in a workspace ZIP, prefixed with the project's number, such as `2-memory.json`; a snapshot's scripts and memory are prefixed `s1-`, `s2-` and so on, and snapshots share asset files with identical contents), so scripts and memory can be read and edited outside Tablecraft; ZIPs from before keep them inside `project.json` and still load; no external file paths are needed. Component ZIPs use the same structure with a component-bundle manifest.
- Import validates the complete hierarchy and all references, and adds copies instead of replacing existing projects. Workspace/project import is disabled during live play to avoid replacing an active room accidentally.
- To host a restored save, load/import it offline and then host a new room. Its seats open up for joining players, who take them over together with their saved hands.
- Keep ZIP backups: browser storage has quotas and can be cleared, especially when importing large assets or keeping many snapshots of a project with large textures. When storage is full, saving a snapshot says so; delete or export some snapshots to make room. Recovery downloads for unreadable browser storage remain raw JSON so the original data is preserved.
- Invalid saves, imports, server commands and storage failures are reported explicitly. Corrupt workspace/snapshot data is not automatically overwritten; the UI offers a recovery download.

There is no fixed player limit. Guardrails limit a project to 500 components (including imported assets), 10,000 pieces, 300 layers per face, and 10,000 CSV rows per component; individual spawn commands create at most 1,000 pieces. Each imported asset is limited to 5 MiB, with 20 MiB of assets per project. ZIP files are limited to 60 MiB compressed and 80 MiB expanded. Browser-local quotas and live-server message limits may be lower; failures are reported explicitly. Snapshots are capped at 100.

# Playground undo

Use **Undo** in the header's **Actions** menu, or **Ctrl+Z / Cmd+Z**, to reverse the latest tabletop action, and **Redo** (**Ctrl+Y**, or **Ctrl+Shift+Z / Cmd+Shift+Z**) to bring back what you undid, one step at a time; redone actions can be undone again. Doing anything new on the table forgets what could be redone. A script function call is one action, memory included (see Scripting for changes scripts keep out of undo). In a live room, **any authenticated player can undo the most recent action by any player**, and everyone receives the result. Undo includes pieces, stacks, dice rolls, hands, table settings, and edits to existing seats. Shortcuts do not intercept typing, dialogs, pass-device curtains, or Component designer editing. The inspector's Rotation, Scale, Grid size and selected-seat fields apply their value when you finish typing (blur or Enter), release a scrub or slider, or close the color picker, so each finished edit is one action. Redo works the same way: in a live room anyone seated can redo the latest undone action, for everyone.

The latest 50 effective actions are retained. Offline history lasts until a workspace reload, replacement/import or live-session transition; it is not included in ZIP exports or snapshots. Live room history is stored server-side with the room and survives reconnects. A component design edit/removal or explicit new-seat creation clears history and displays a notice; designs and seat identities/ownership are never restored by undo. Joining an existing live room, or taking and leaving seats, does not clear history; undo never removes a seat.
